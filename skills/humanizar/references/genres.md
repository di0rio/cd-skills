# Conventions by genre

Each genre has a different way of sounding human. Humanizing an article doesn't mean making it casual; humanizing a post doesn't mean making it academic.

## LinkedIn and social posts

- **The first line is the one that matters.** It shows before "see more". Start with the fact, the result or the concrete situation, not "I'm thrilled to announce".
- **Tell what happened, not the lesson.** "I spent 3 weeks thinking the bug was in the database. It was a time zone." beats "I learned that debugging takes patience".
- **First person, short sentences, paragraphs of 1 to 3 lines.** That's the convention of the medium; keep it.
- **One lesson, if any, at the end.** No "5 takeaways" list.
- **Emoji: zero or one.** Hashtags: up to 3, if the user wants them.
- **No generic engagement question** ("What do you think?"). If you ask, ask something specific.
- Gratitude: thank people by name and for what they did, not "grateful to everyone who was part of this journey".

## Portfolio, bio, "about me", project description

- **What the project does in one sentence, no adjectives.** "App that splits a bar tab by what each person ordered." Not "innovative and intuitive solution for shared expense management".
- **Why it exists.** The real problem, ideally personal or concrete.
- **Technical decisions with a reason.** "Used PGlite to run without a local Postgres" says more than "modern and robust stack".
- **Numbers, if there are any.** Users, time, size, performance. No number, no superlative.
- **First-person bios** sound more human than third person ("John is a passionate developer..."), unless the site calls for third person.
- Cut "passionate about technology", "always looking for new challenges", "proactive", "resilient". Show it with a fact.
- **Website bio (home, "about"):** 2 to 4 sentences. What you do and where, one detail only you would say (a concrete preference, a habit, a project), and stop. Contact links live in the site's buttons; don't repeat them in prose ("find me on GitHub, on LinkedIn...") if they're right below.
- **Job title and seniority the way the person uses them.** If they introduce themselves as a "junior dev", don't promote them to "engineer" or "expert", even if the reference does.

## Website and landing copy (tagline, caption, button, section)

- **A tagline says what it is, not how good it is.** "React components you install into your project", not "the best way to build interfaces".
- **A caption explains what the reader is looking at**, with the detail they wouldn't notice on their own ("the old video jumped from component to component"). It doesn't repeat the heading.
- **Buttons and labels: verb + object, short.** "watch the videos", "copy command". No "Learn more" when you can say what.
- **Numbers with honest context.** "191/200 on a 5-task test, one run" beats a bare "191/200"; and don't hide the limitation.
- **At most one punchline per page**, and only if it's in the author's voice. A product landing page can carry more personality than a bio, but a slogan in every section gets tiring.

## Essays (school, admissions, exams)

- The essay structure is **required**: an introduction with a thesis, body paragraphs with arguments, a conclusion. Don't break it.
- What makes it human: **specific evidence** (data, a law, an author, a historical fact, correctly named) instead of "according to experts"; **varied connectives, used only where there's a real logical link**; **a clear thesis that takes a position**.
- Avoid the stock formulas graders have seen a thousand times ("Since the dawn of humanity", "In today's society", "In conclusion, it is clear that"). In Brazilian Portuguese, "Diante do exposto", "Hodiernamente", "Destarte", "É mister", "Nesse viés".
- A concrete proposal (who, what, how, what for), not "the government should raise awareness".
- Don't invent quotes or data. If the user didn't provide evidence, suggest the kinds of evidence they could look for.

## Academic writing (paper, thesis, abstract, report)

- **Keep the formal register**, impersonal if the norm requires it. Humanizing here means **clarity**, not informality.
- Keep subject and verb close. Cut chains of nominalizations ("the carrying out of the analysis of the implementation" becomes "we analyzed how they implemented it").
- Claims proportional to the evidence. "The data suggest" when they suggest; "the data show" when they show. No double hedging.
- Introduction: the problem, the gap, what this work does. No "Since the dawn of" or "In contemporary society".
- Conclusion: what was found, limitations, next step. Don't repeat the introduction.
- "Plays a pivotal role", "of paramount importance", "it is worth noting", "in this context" are the most flagged tics in generated academic text.
- **Never change citations, references, data or results.** Never add a source. When rewriting, keep each citation tied to the same claim.

## Email, messages, README (when they come up)

- Subject line or first sentence with the request or the main information.
- No "I hope this email finds you well".
- README: what it is, how to run it, in that order. No marketing paragraph before `install`.
